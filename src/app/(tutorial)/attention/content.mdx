# Paying Attention

<Lead>
In the last chapter, we saw that predicting the next word requires understanding, but our models could only look at a fixed window of recent words. What if the model could *choose* which earlier words to focus on? That idea, called attention, is the breakthrough that makes modern AI work. And it's built entirely from things you already know.
</Lead>

## Some Words Need Other Words

You often can't tell what a word means without looking at other words in the sentence. The word "bank" could be a place for money or the side of a river. You need to see the words around it to know which one. A pronoun like "it" makes no sense on its own. You have to look back and find what it refers to.

This is something our fixed-window model from the last chapter can't do well. It sees the last few words and has no way to reach back and pick out the *specific* earlier words that matter.

Each of these cases needs the same ability: given a word, *choose* which other words in the sentence are relevant to understanding it. Not the nearest words. Not a fixed window. The *right* words, wherever they happen to be.

This is what **attention** does. It gives the model the ability to look at every word that came before and decide which ones matter for the word it's currently processing. Before we build it out of vectors, it helps to see that you already do this kind of thing all the time.

## Looking Things Up

Think about what you do when you need to find something out. You have a question in mind, and you go looking for a source that can answer it.

Say you want to know how tall Mount Everest is. You might pull one book off the shelf, the one whose spine says "Mountains of the World." You picked it by its cover, without reading every page of every book in the house. Or you type "height of Everest" into a search engine, which checks what it has on file about each page and hands back the one that fits. Or you ask the friend you know is into hiking.

Every version of this has the same three parts:

- **The query** is what you're looking for. Your question.
- **The key** is what each source advertises about itself. The title on the spine, the words a search engine keeps on file for a page, the topic a friend is known for.
- **The value** is what you actually get once you pick a source. The words inside the book, the page that loads, what your friend tells you.

You match your **query** against each **key** to decide where to look, then you collect the **value** from whatever matched best.

|  | Query (what you're looking for) | Key (what each source advertises) | Value (what you get back) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A book** | the answer you need | the title and cover | what's written inside |
| **A web search** | the words you type | what the engine has on file for each page | the page that comes back |
| **A friend** | your question | what they're known for knowing | everything they could tell you |

The query and the key are different kinds of thing, and that difference matters. What you're looking for is a question. What a book advertises is a topic. You never mix the two up. Attention keeps them separate for the same reason.

## Words Look Things Up Too

A word in a sentence does the same thing. To pin down what it means, it has a question it needs answered, and it goes looking through the other words for the answer.

Take "I dropped the glass and it broke." The word "it" needs to know what it stands for. So "it" carries a query, roughly "what thing was just mentioned?" The word "glass" carries a key that says "I'm a thing that was just mentioned." Those match. The value "it" collects from "glass" is the glass itself, so now "it" knows what it means.

Or "The chef who won the competition opened a restaurant." The word "opened" needs to know who did the opening. Its query is "who performed this action?" The word "chef" advertises a key, "I'm someone who acts," and "competition" does not. So "opened" pulls its answer from "chef."

Each time, the word asks one question, finds the word whose key fits, and gathers a value. The result is a fuller understanding of the asking word: "it" filled in with "glass," "opened" tied to "chef."

That is the whole idea of attention. Everything left is making "asks a question" and "the key fits" precise enough for a computer to run. For that, we go back to vectors.

## Matching Queries with Keys

We can make "asks a question" exact with a tool from the Vectors chapter: the **dot product**, which measures how much two vectors agree.

Write each token's query as a vector and each token's key as a vector. To score how well a query matches a key, take their dot product. A big score means a strong match. A score near zero means no match. (The token isn't literally asking anything; the model does the asking on its behalf. The math is just easier to follow from the token's point of view.)

This is the same idea as the detector from the Vectors chapter. A query is a detector that says "I'm looking for this kind of thing." A key describes what a token is. The dot product measures how strongly the key sets off the detector.

In a real model the keys and queries are vectors with many dimensions, one for each kind of thing a word might look for or advertise. To watch the machinery without drowning in numbers, we'll shrink it to the simplest possible case: a single dimension that means "noun." A key of 1 means "I am a noun," a key of 0 means "I'm not." A query of 1 means "I'm looking for a noun." Real queries and keys hunt for many things at once. This one looks for exactly one.

Imagine a tiny vocabulary of four tokens: **cat**, **dog**, **blah** (a filler word), and **it** (a pronoun). "cat" and "dog" offer themselves as nouns. "blah" offers nothing. "it" is looking for a noun. Multiply "it"'s query by each token's key and you get a match score for each one. The bigger the score, the better the match.

What you offer isn't the same as what you're looking for. A pronoun needs to find nouns without being mistaken for one itself. That's why keys and queries are separate.

## Dividing Your Attention

Match scores are useful, but the model can't just keep piling them up. Each token has a fixed budget of attention, 100%, and needs to *divide* that budget among all the candidates. We need to turn raw scores into percentages that add up to 1. You'll find out why the attention needs to add up to 100% later.

The function usually used for this is called **softmax**. Think of it as a competition: bigger numbers dominate, smaller numbers get squeezed, and the result is a set of percentages.

<SoftmaxExplorerWidget>
Drag the sliders. Try "All equal" — everyone gets 25%. Try "Moderate, rest silent" — A wins by default because nobody else is saying anything. Try "Moderate meets loud" — B drowns out A. The gaps between scores matter more than the absolute values. Softmax cares about who's loudest *relative to everyone else*.
</SoftmaxExplorerWidget>

Now apply softmax to the dot products we computed above. With keys and queries at 1/0, "it" against `cat blah blah it` gets scores `[1, 0, 0, 0]`, which softmax turns into roughly `[47.5%, 17.5%, 17.5%, 17.5%]`. That's the right shape, cat wins, but more than half the attention leaks to the filler tokens. Not great.

The fix is to crank up the *query magnitude*. This is the detector-sensitivity lever from the Vectors chapter: a longer query vector is a more sensitive detector, so the gap between a match and a miss grows. The keys still describe what each token is, but the asker now cares much more about the tokens that matched best. With "it"'s query at 10 instead of 1, the scores become `[10, 0, 0, 0]`, and softmax turns those into roughly `[99.99%, ≈0%, ≈0%, ≈0%]`. Nearly all the attention goes to the noun.

<ToyAttentionSoftmaxWidget>
Toggle the query magnitude between 1 and 10. At 1, attention leaks all over the place. At 10, "it" puts almost all of its attention on the noun. There's still a tiny leak — softmax always allocates 100%, so a fraction of a percent goes to every loser. That's honest and we'll come back to it.
</ToyAttentionSoftmaxWidget>

But there's a bigger problem. Try clicking on "blah" in the widget above. "blah" doesn't have a noun-finding question — its query is just zero. So every score is zero, and softmax spreads attention evenly across all four tokens. The widget reports "blah pays 25% attention to itself, 25% to cat, 25% to the other blah, 25% to it," which is meaningless. The same thing happens to "it" if there are no nouns in the sentence. Softmax has nothing to grab onto, so it spreads attention everywhere, with full confidence.

## Where Does Attention Go When Nothing Matches?

Softmax always allocates 100% somewhere, even when no real match is available. We need a sensible place for the leftover attention to go.

The fix is to add a **sink** token whose only job is to absorb leftover attention. Give it a key that's *slightly* nounish — say, `[0.5]` — so it scores higher than zero, but lower than a real noun like `[1]`. When a real noun is around, the noun wins by a wide margin, because of the cranked-up query magnitude. When no noun is around, the sink wins by default, since `0.5` is the only score above zero.

That handles the case where "it" can't find a noun. The other case, an off-task word like "blah" that isn't searching for a noun at all, needs one more touch: the sink also advertises a generic "nothing in particular" signal, and off-task words point their query at exactly that. So a word with no real question still has somewhere harmless to land, and the sink is the only token that answers it.

<ToyAttentionSinkWidget>

Try `blah blah blah it` — there's no noun to find, and the sink wins ~97% of the attention. Try clicking "blah" — it isn't looking for a noun, so its attention parks on the sink (also ~97%). Every off-task token now has somewhere harmless to land.
</ToyAttentionSinkWidget>

Real models don't always use a dedicated sink token. Sometimes the model learns to use a punctuation mark as a sink, or a start-of-sentence token, or just has the token attend to itself. The idea is the same in every case: when this head doesn't have anything useful to do for a particular token, attention has to go somewhere harmless.

## What Did You Find?

So far attention tells us *where to look*, which tokens are relevant. But we also need *what to gather* from those tokens.

Each token gets a third vector: a **value**. The query and key decide who talks to whom. The value is what gets said. For our pronoun example, the value encodes which animal the token represents: cat's value is `[1, 0]`, dog's value is `[0, 1]`, and filler tokens (including the sink) have nothing to share, so their value is `[0, 0]`.

Attention then blends these values using the softmax weights as a recipe. If "it" pays nearly 100% attention to "cat" and a fraction of a percent everywhere else, the result is roughly `[1, 0]`, almost pure cat.

<ToyAttentionValuesWidget>
Click "it" in `cat blah blah it` — the result is almost all cat. Try `cat blah dog it` — half cat, half dog, because "it" matched both nouns equally and the model can't tell which. Try `dog blah dog it` — *two* dogs, and the result is even sharper than with just one. Try `blah blah blah it` — there's no noun to find, attention falls on the sink, and the result vector is empty. That's exactly what "nothing useful gathered" looks like.
</ToyAttentionValuesWidget>

Two of the same noun produces a *more* confident answer than one, not a doubled one. Softmax-weighted blending stays in the same value-space as the input, no matter how many matches there are. Match dog twice, you get dog. Match dog and cat, you get half-dog-half-cat. The mechanism never produces "double dog."

When attention falls on the sink (whose value is `[0, 0]`), the result is empty. That's a clean way to represent "this head had nothing useful to say."

## Where the Vectors Come From

We've been talking about query, key, and value vectors, but where do they actually come from?

The answer is the same one we've run into many times before. The model uses gradient descent to learn the weights that make it perform best and thus minimize its error.

To be more precise, the model learns a simple one-layer neural network (without an activation function) that transforms the token's current embedding into query, key, and value vectors. Nobody hand-writes "this word advertises nounness." The model discovers, from its training data, which questions are worth asking and how each word should answer them.

And the model doesn't stop at one set of these weights. It learns several, side by side, which is the last piece of the picture.

## Multi-Headed Attention

Go back to "bank." To work out which kind of bank it is, the word really wants to ask two different questions: "is there a river nearby?" and "is there money nearby?" A single query looks for one thing, so one round of matching can't cover both.

A word often has more than one question like this. The answer is to let it ask several, each in its own separate round of attention. An **attention head** is one such round: one set of queries, keys, and values, learning one pattern of who attends to whom. A model runs many heads in parallel, and each is free to learn a different question. This is called **multi-headed attention**. Each head produces its own version of every token, and those versions are combined into one that gets passed on.

It's like having several friends. You don't ask all your questions of one person. You ask the hiking friend about trails, the cooking friend about recipes, the doctor friend about that weird rash. Each friend is a separate place to take a different question. Each head works the same way: a separate venue where a word can ask one kind of question and get one kind of answer. Run enough of them and a single word can chase down everything it needs at once, what it refers to, who acted, which clause it belongs in.

<KeyInsight>
Multiple attention heads let the model ask many different questions in parallel. Each head has its own query, key, and value weights, so each learns to attend to a different kind of relationship: pronouns, grammar, structure, semantics. **The model doesn't need to choose one kind of attention; it gets all of them at once.**
</KeyInsight>

In real models, every head also has its own sink behavior, because no single head can apply to every token in every sentence. A pronoun-resolving head will be on-task for pronouns and idle for everything else. That idle behavior is what the sink is for.

## What We've Built

Attention is:

1. **Query, key, value** — Each token asks a question (query), advertises what it is (key), and offers something to share (value), exactly like looking information up.
2. **Match scores** — Each query and key combine via dot product to score how relevant each token is to each other token.
3. **Softmax** — Turn relevance scores into percentages that add up to 100%.
4. **The sink** — A place for attention to go when nothing relevant is around, so off-task tokens have somewhere harmless to land.
5. **Values blended** — The value vectors get blended together using the softmax weights.
6. **Multiple heads** — Run many attention patterns in parallel, each specializing in a different relationship.

But our attention mechanism has no idea *where* words are, only *what* they are. "The dog chased the cat" and "the cat chased the dog" produce identical attention scores. In the next chapter, we'll fix this with a trick involving rotation.
