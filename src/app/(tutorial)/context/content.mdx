# Getting the Right Information

<Lead>
Models can only see so much at once. This chapter looks at how to make the most of that limited window, and how to fill it with the information that actually matters.
</Lead>

## The Wall

<div>
In [Chapter 7](/attention), attention let every word look at every other word, and decide how much to listen to each one. That is what makes attention so good at connecting distant words: a pronoun on page ten can still reach back to the name it refers to on page one. But comparing every word to every other word has a cost, and that cost grows fast.

Double the number of words in the context, and the number of pairs to compare quadruples. Ten times the words means a hundred times the comparisons. This is quadratic growth, and it applies separately to every layer of the model, and again for every new word it generates.

A page of text is a few hundred words. A book is tens of thousands. A large codebase can run past a million lines. Push the context out to a million words, and full attention needs on the order of a trillion comparisons, per layer, per new word generated. Yet models today read whole books and codebases in one go. Something has to give.
</div>

## Don't Redo the Past

<div>
A model generates one word at a time. Each new word attends back over every word that came before it, including the words it just generated itself. Ask for a hundred words of output, and the last word needs the keys and values of all ninety-nine before it.

Recomputing those keys and values from scratch at every step is wasteful. The keys and values for a word never change once that word is written, so a model can compute them once and store them: the KV cache. Generating the next word then only costs a single new set of keys and values, reused against everything already stored.

The catch is memory. The cache holds one entry per word in the prompt plus every word generated so far, and it only grows. That growing cache is the real cost of a long context, and it shapes several of the tricks in the rest of this chapter.
</div>

<KVCacheWidget>
Generate a dozen words with reuse off, then on. Watch how without the cache the model redoes all its earlier work every single step, while the cache turns that into one small step per word.
</KVCacheWidget>

## Most Words Don't Need Most Words

<div>
Full attention lets every word look at every other word, but most words don't need most of that. A word usually gets what it needs from its neighbors: the words right around it, a paragraph back at most. Only a few layers in the whole stack need to reach all the way across the text.

Gemma 3 builds on that. Five out of every six layers are local: each word attends only to a small window of nearby words. The sixth layer is global, attending to the entire context. Gemma 2 split the difference differently, alternating one local layer for every global layer, with a 4096-word window. Gemma 3 pushes further, five local layers to one global, with a 1024-word window on the local layers.

Local layers are far cheaper: a word compares against a window instead of the whole sequence, so the cost scales with the window size instead of the square of the context length. That shrinks the KV cache too, since a local layer only ever needs to keep the nearby entries in memory. Stacking several local layers still lets information travel a long way, because each layer passes what it has gathered a little further, and those overlapping windows add up.
</div>

<LocalVsGlobalWidget>
Set every layer to Global and check the cost. Now switch to the Gemma pattern: five cheap local layers, one global. A word can still reach clear across the text, because each local layer passes information a little further than the last.
</LocalVsGlobalWidget>

## Pick the Words That Matter

<div>
Local attention shrinks the window by making each layer look at fewer words by default. Sparse attention takes a different approach: keep the whole window, but let a small, fast model decide which words in it actually matter for each new word being generated, and run full attention on only that shortlist.

That fast model is an indexer. It scans every earlier word and gives each one a relevance score for the word currently being generated, the way a search engine ranks pages before anyone reads one. Full attention then runs only on the top-scoring words. Scoring one word is cheap: the indexer is small and works at low precision, nothing like the cost of comparing that word in full attention. But it still has to score every word in the context, not just the shortlist, so at a million words that scan adds up to a real cost of its own. That's the cost IndexShare goes after.

DeepSeek's DSA (DeepSeek Sparse Attention) does this with what it calls a lightning indexer, keeping the top 2,048 words out of a context window that can hold 128,000. Full attention only ever runs on those 2,048, no matter how long the document gets.

GLM-5.2 pushes the idea further with something it calls IndexShare. Neighboring layers tend to want nearly the same words: if a word matters to layer 12, it usually matters to layer 13 as well. Every layer scanning the whole context on its own is exactly the kind of cost that piles up at a million words, so instead of running the indexer at every layer, GLM-5.2 runs it once every four layers and reuses that shortlist for the three layers after it. That adds up to about 2.9 times fewer computations than recomputing the indexer at every layer, and it's a big part of what makes a million-word context window practical at close to frontier quality.

Neither Claude nor OpenAI's models disclose how they handle long context internally. GLM-5.2 is an open model with performance in a similar range, and it documents its method in a public paper, so it's reasonable to assume the closed labs are doing something broadly similar under the hood.
</div>

<KeyInsight>
A trillion comparisons per layer, per word, is not something any model actually does at a million words of context. A cheap indexer that scores every word, combined with a shortlist that several layers can share instead of recomputing, is what turns that impossible number into a manageable one.
</KeyInsight>

## Memory in Pages

<div>
The KV cache from earlier in this chapter is the real memory hog of a long context: one entry per word, for every word in the prompt and everything generated since. Serving many requests at once means holding many of these caches side by side, and the simplest way to do that is to reserve one block of memory per request, sized for the longest sequence that request might ever reach. Most requests never get that long, so most of that reserved memory sits empty the whole time.

vLLM's PagedAttention borrows an old idea from operating systems instead. An OS doesn't give a program one giant reserved slab of memory; it splits memory into small fixed-size pages and hands a program only the pages it actually uses, wherever they happen to be free, keeping a table that maps the program's view of memory onto those scattered physical pages. PagedAttention does the same thing to the KV cache: split it into small fixed blocks, maybe sixteen words each, and keep a block table per sequence that points each of its logical blocks at wherever that block actually lives in physical memory. A block is a page, a word is a byte, a request is a program.

Blocks don't need to sit next to each other, and a sequence only holds as many blocks as it currently needs — one partly-empty block at the end, not a whole reserved chunk. That turns wasted memory from most of the cache into almost none. It also opens up something the reserved-block scheme can't do: if two sequences share an identical stretch, such as the same system prompt, they can point their block tables at the very same physical blocks instead of each keeping their own copy.
</div>
