<Question>
In this chapter, "error" means:

<Choice correct>The distance from what you have to perfect</Choice>
<Choice>A typo or mistake in the model's output</Choice>
<Choice>A computer crash during a training run</Choice>
<Choice>A parameter set to the wrong value</Choice>

<Feedback>
*Error* is a number measuring how far off you are from a perfect result — a 28-point gap to a perfect 100 on a test, or a 14.2-second gap to a perfect race time of zero. Optimization is the work of making that number smaller.
</Feedback>

<Question>
What does the *gradient* tell you?

<Choice>How far off you are from the right answer</Choice>
<Choice>The total number of parameters in the model</Choice>
<Choice correct>Which direction reduces the error fastest</Choice>
<Choice>Roughly how long training will take</Choice>

<Feedback>
The gradient is an arrow pointing in the steepest downhill direction across all parameters at once. It says which way to nudge each knob.

(A) describes the *error*, not the gradient. The gradient adds direction on top of magnitude — and that's what makes optimizing millions of knobs at once even possible.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
The chapter says the human eye, the Wright brothers' airplane, and the iPhone were all "iterated into existence" rather than designed from scratch. What's the takeaway?

<Choice>Smart designers are overrated — anyone could have made these things</Choice>
<Choice correct>Complex systems emerge through small improvements, not design</Choice>
<Choice>Modern AI is just a copy of biological evolution</Choice>
<Choice>Complex things always take billions of years to develop</Choice>

<Feedback>
Each of these — biological organisms, airplanes, smartphones — was built up through generations of tiny tweaks tested against reality. The pattern is universal: complex things almost never come from one brilliant design.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Why doesn't the chapter's "try a small change, keep it if it helped" algorithm work for guessing a 6-character password?

<Choice>Passwords are randomly chosen, so optimization can't help</Choice>
<Choice>Passwords are encrypted before being stored on disk</Choice>
<Choice>Each character has too many possibilities to try</Choice>
<Choice correct>You can't tell if a tweak brings you closer</Choice>

<Feedback>
A password checker is *non-smooth*: any guess that isn't exactly right comes back as "wrong", telling you nothing about how close you were. Without a "warmer/colder" signal, small tweaks give you nothing to follow.

(C) is tempting — the search space *is* huge — but huge search spaces are fine when the function is smooth. The killer here is the missing signal, not the size.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Someone argues: "For AI to do something impressive — like writing a story — engineers must have figured out the rules of good writing and programmed them in." What does the chapter say is wrong?

<Choice correct>The model learns to do it without being explicitly taught</Choice>
<Choice>AI can't actually write good stories yet</Choice>
<Choice>AI uses a separate, secret algorithm for writing tasks</Choice>
<Choice>Stories follow simpler rules than people think</Choice>

<Feedback>
Nobody writes down "the rules of good writing" for the model. Training just shows it lots of examples and adjusts parameters incrementally until the output is good — the model picks up the patterns of good writing on its own, without ever being told what those patterns are.

(B) is a common stance, but the issue isn't AI's current quality — it's how AI is *built*. Even when AI does something impressive, the impressive part wasn't designed in; it was learned.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
According to the chapter, what enabled modern AI to finally work — given that "the basic ideas have been around for decades"?

<Choice>The discovery of a fundamentally new optimization principle</Choice>
<Choice correct>More data, more compute, and incremental model improvements</Choice>
<Choice>A complete redesign of how computers internally calculate</Choice>
<Choice>Quantum computers running thousands of times faster</Choice>

<Feedback>
The basic algorithm — incremental optimization on smooth models — has been around for decades. What changed recently was having enough data to measure error on, enough compute to run billions of tiny adjustments, and models that have gradually improved through many small architectural tweaks. New architectures (like transformers) absolutely matter — but they're incremental refinements within the same paradigm of gradient descent on smooth functions, not a brand-new principle of learning.
</Feedback>
</Question>
