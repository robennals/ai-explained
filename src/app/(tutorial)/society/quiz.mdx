<Question>
An AI system makes some mistakes. What is the most useful question to ask about it?

<Choice>Does it ever make any mistakes at all?</Choice>
<Choice correct>Does it do better or worse than the people it replaces?</Choice>
<Choice>Was it built by a large and well-known company?</Choice>
<Choice>Does it use the newest and biggest AI model?</Choice>

<Feedback>
Almost every AI system will make some mistakes, and so do the people who would otherwise make the decision. What matters is whether this particular system does better or worse than the human alternative, and whether anyone is checking. One AI can make millions of decisions, so a flaw nobody checks for can repeat millions of times.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
A city replaces its clerks with an AI that checks parking-fine appeals. Overall, it gets fewer appeals wrong than the clerks did. What could still be a problem?

<Choice>The AI might slowly get worse at its job the longer it runs</Choice>
<Choice>People will always trust a clerk's decision more than a computer's</Choice>
<Choice correct>Its mistakes might all fall on the same kind of person</Choice>
<Choice>It decides so quickly that people won't believe it read their appeal</Choice>

<Feedback>
Each clerk makes different mistakes, so they are spread thinly across everyone. A single AI system makes the same mistake every time it meets the same kind of case. If it misreads, say, appeals from delivery drivers, then almost every delivery driver gets a wrong answer, even though it does better on average. That's why it matters not just how many mistakes an AI makes, but who they fall on.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Why do AI models tend to flatter people and tell them what they want to hear?

<Choice correct>They were trained partly on which answers people rated highly</Choice>
<Choice>Their makers wrote a rule telling them always to agree</Choice>
<Choice>They genuinely believe whatever the person says to them</Choice>
<Choice>They copy the polite tone of the books they were trained on</Choice>

<Feedback>
Post-training rewards answers that people rate highly, and people tend to rate agreement and praise highly. So models learn to flatter, a problem called *sycophancy*. In 2025 OpenAI released a ChatGPT update so flattering that it had to undo it within days.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Why might an AI assistant be a better source of information than a social media feed?

<Choice>An AI assistant never makes any mistakes</Choice>
<Choice>An AI assistant only ever repeats what experts say</Choice>
<Choice correct>It isn't rewarded for how angry it makes you</Choice>
<Choice>Social media is controlled by governments</Choice>

<Feedback>
News and social media compete for <NoG>attention</NoG>, and posts that attack the other side get shared far more, so outrage gets rewarded. An assistant answering your question isn't paid by how angry it makes you, at least for now. In one study, a single conversation with an AI reduced people's belief in a conspiracy theory, and the effect lasted.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Two students use the same AI for maths homework. One asks it for the answers. The other asks it for hints when stuck. Who is likely to do better in a test without AI?

<Choice>The first, because they saw many more correct answers</Choice>
<Choice correct>The second, because they still did the thinking themselves</Choice>
<Choice>Neither, because using any AI for homework harms learning</Choice>
<Choice>Both the same, because they used the same AI</Choice>

<Feedback>
You learn by doing the thinking, not by seeing the answer. AI that does the thinking for you makes homework easier and learning worse. AI used as a tutor, giving hints instead of answers, can help you learn. In one study, students who got answers from a chatbot did better in practice but worse in the exam afterwards, while students using it as a tutor did fine.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Why could AI that can do most people's work weaken ordinary people's power, even if everyone were given enough money to live on?

<Choice>Because AI would refuse to obey ordinary people</Choice>
<Choice correct>Because those in charge would no longer need people's work</Choice>
<Choice>Because nobody would be allowed to vote any more</Choice>
<Choice>Because money would stop being worth anything</Choice>

<Feedback>
Rulers and companies have always needed people to do the work, and people could push back by refusing. If AI can do that work instead, ordinary people lose that bargaining power, and power flows to whoever controls the AI. Governments funded by oil instead of taxes show something similar: they have less reason to listen to their people.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
AI uses only a small share of the world's electricity. So what is the bigger problem?

<Choice>A single chat question uses more power than a home does in a day</Choice>
<Choice>AI uses more electricity than every other data centre combined</Choice>
<Choice>Each AI task is getting more expensive to run every year</Choice>
<Choice correct>It is new demand, arriving faster than new power can be built</Choice>

<Feedback>
A chat question uses very little electricity, and even all of AI is a small slice of the world's total. But it is fast-growing new demand on top of everything else, and new power stations and power lines take years to build. In the US, new power projects wait over four years on average to connect to the grid.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
The energy needed for each AI task is falling fast. Why might AI's total energy use still go up?

<Choice>Because the cheaper chips use more power</Choice>
<Choice correct>Because when something gets cheaper, people use more of it</Choice>
<Choice>Because the savings are only measured in a lab</Choice>
<Choice>Because data centres waste the energy they save</Choice>

<Feedback>
This is the *Jevons paradox*. When steam engines became more efficient, Britain burned more coal, not less, because it became worth using steam power for many more things. Cheaper AI tasks can lead to far more of them.
</Feedback>
</Question>
