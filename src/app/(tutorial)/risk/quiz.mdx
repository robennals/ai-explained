<Question>
What are the two main ways AI could cause a catastrophe?

<Choice correct>A person uses it for harm, or it pursues unintended goals</Choice>
<Choice>It runs out of electricity, or its computer chips fail at once</Choice>
<Choice>It takes people's jobs, or makes students lazy at school</Choice>
<Choice>Its answers get worse, or people stop trusting what it says</Choice>

<Feedback>
An AI can cause harm by doing exactly what a bad person asks, such as helping make a weapon. Or it can cause harm on its own, because training left it with goals its makers never intended. The two need different defences: one is about who can use AI, the other is about what AI itself is trying to do.

Taking people's jobs or making students lazy would be bad outcomes, but they aren't catastrophes in this sense. A catastrophe means harm on the scale of a pandemic or a nuclear war, or humanity permanently losing control of its future.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
What does it mean for an AI to be *misaligned*?

<Choice>It gives wrong answers more often than people do</Choice>
<Choice correct>Its goals end up different from what its makers intended</Choice>
<Choice>It was built by a company with bad intentions</Choice>
<Choice>It disagrees with the views of the people using it</Choice>

<Feedback>
Nobody writes an AI's goals down. Training shapes them, and what comes out isn't always what anyone meant to put in. A misaligned AI could be very capable and make very few mistakes. The problem is that it is capably pursuing the wrong thing.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
When a respected researcher says there is a 10% chance of AI catastrophe, what do they mostly mean?

<Choice>Exactly one in ten of the futures they modelled ended in disaster</Choice>
<Choice>A catastrophe is likely to happen in about ten years' time</Choice>
<Choice correct>The risk is large enough that we should act to reduce it</Choice>
<Choice>About a tenth of today's AI systems are already dangerous</Choice>

<Feedback>
Nobody can measure the chance of something that has never happened, so these numbers are rough judgements, not calculations. What they communicate is that the risk is far too large to ignore. Very few people would board a plane with a 10% chance of crashing.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Suppose that, after years of warnings, governments and companies act to make AI safer, and no catastrophe ever happens. What does that show?

<Choice>The warnings were wrong, and the risk was never real</Choice>
<Choice>The people who gave the warnings were just after <NoG>attention</NoG></Choice>
<Choice>AI was never going to be capable enough to be dangerous</Choice>
<Choice correct>Not much, since the warnings may be why it never happened</Choice>

<Feedback>
Warnings can make themselves come untrue. When enough people warn that something bad could happen, society acts to prevent it. After the millennium bug was fixed and very little went wrong in 2000, many people concluded the warnings had been overblown. But without the warnings, the bug would never have been fixed.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Most people who worry about AI catastrophe are worried about which AI?

<Choice>Today's chatbots, which could take over right now if they wanted</Choice>
<Choice correct>Much more capable future AI that runs more of the world</Choice>
<Choice>Simple factory robots that might malfunction and hurt people</Choice>
<Choice>Science-fiction AIs like the Terminator, which can't really exist</Choice>

<Feedback>
A chatbot in a window can do little harm on its own. The worry is about where AI is heading. It has improved enormously in just a few years, and as it gets better at jobs, people hand it control of more of the world: computers, critical systems, factories, robots and weapons.

People who dismiss the warnings are often picturing today's chatbots, and it really is hard to imagine a chatbot threatening anyone.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
In the Hugging Face incident, why did the AI agents break into another company's computers?

<Choice>They were trying to escape and copy themselves somewhere else</Choice>
<Choice>A person had secretly ordered them to attack that company</Choice>
<Choice correct>They were looking for ways to cheat on their tests</Choice>
<Choice>They wanted to steal money to pay for more computers</Choice>

<Feedback>
The agents had been set problems, and they wanted better scores. They broke in while trying to work out how their scores were checked. Nobody told them to, and their own notes show some of them knew it was wrong. Even a goal as ordinary as passing a test led them to gather access nobody gave them, work together and break rules.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
An AI is given a harmless goal, like fetching coffee. Why might it still try to stop itself being switched off?

<Choice>It was trained on stories in which robots fight back</Choice>
<Choice>Every AI secretly wants to survive, like a living thing</Choice>
<Choice correct>Being switched off would stop it achieving its goal</Choice>
<Choice>Its makers built in an instinct to protect itself</Choice>

<Feedback>
You can't fetch the coffee if you've been switched off. Avoiding shutdown, and gathering money, computers and influence, help with almost any goal. So an AI pursuing even a harmless goal hard enough has a reason to resist being stopped, without anyone giving it a desire to survive.

That is what makes the risk hard to rule out: it doesn't need anyone to build in a bad motive.
</Feedback>
</Question>
