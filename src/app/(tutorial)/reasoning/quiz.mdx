<Question>
What actually changes in a reasoning model?

<Choice correct>It writes hidden working before it answers</Choice>
<Choice>A new memory component is added to it</Choice>
<Choice>Attention is replaced by a search algorithm</Choice>
<Choice>It runs the question through the model many times</Choice>

<Feedback>
The architecture does not change at all. The model writes tokens into a thinking section the product hides, reads them back as context, and then writes the answer. Its own output is its working memory. What made this work was training with automatically checkable answers, which let the reward go to whatever scratchpad led to a correct result.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Why does writing its working out make a model better at hard questions?

<Choice>The thinking tokens run through a bigger model</Choice>
<Choice correct>More tokens means more computation to spend</Choice>
<Choice>It looks up solved problems and copies the method</Choice>
<Choice>The question goes through the model several times</Choice>

<Feedback>
A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token, the same number of layers whether the question is easy or hard. A question needing ten steps does not get ten times the computation just for being harder. Writing the working out gets around that: every token of working is more computation, and the model reads back what it has written as it goes, so its own output becomes its scratch paper.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
A model's thinking trace:

<Choice correct>Drives the answer, without being the whole story of it</Choice>
<Choice>Is written afterwards, to explain the answer</Choice>
<Choice>Records which training examples were retrieved</Choice>
<Choice>Is a proof that the final answer is correct</Choice>

<Feedback>
The model writes the trace, reads it back, and answers from it, so the working is real working and it changes the answer. What it does not guarantee is completeness. In experiments where a hint about the answer is slipped into the prompt, models often take the hint and then write a confident chain of reasoning that never mentions it. People do the same thing: the reasons given for a conclusion are not always the reasons that produced it.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
What does most of the work in training a reasoning model?

<Choice>Experts writing out worked solutions to copy</Choice>
<Choice>A second model marking each line of the working</Choice>
<Choice correct>Many attempts, rewarding the ones that got there</Choice>
<Choice>Searching possible lines of reasoning as you ask it</Choice>

<Feedback>
Worked examples do help, and there are millions of them: the web is full of homework help, textbook solutions and forum answers with the working shown, and curated sets of good reasoning are used in fine-tuning too. What they do not cover is breadth and difficulty, because the problems people write up are the standard ones. The method that scales needs nothing but an answer a computer can mark. The model attempts a problem many times over, whole attempts that reached the right answer are made more likely, and the working itself is never marked at all. Habits like finishing a search survive because attempts containing them get there more often.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Reasoning training improved maths and code far more than most other kinds of question. Why?

<Choice>Maths and code are most of the pre-training text</Choice>
<Choice>Their answers are shorter, so they are easier to learn</Choice>
<Choice>The network has a separate part for symbols</Choice>
<Choice correct>It is easy to tell whether an answer is correct</Choice>

<Feedback>
Run the code, check the arithmetic: it is easy to tell whether an answer is correct, and it costs nothing to find out, which makes the training signal as sharp as it gets. That is not to say nothing else can be judged. Judging is easier than producing almost everywhere, and a person or another model can mark a poem or a plan perfectly well. The marking is just noisier and more arguable there, so the same training pulls in the same direction more weakly.
</Feedback>
</Question>
