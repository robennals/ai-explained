Ask a person a hard question and demand an answer in the next half second, and they will do badly. Not because they cannot solve it, but because they need time to think through the problem.

A transformer has the same problem. Each token gets a fixed amount of computation, a fixed number of layers. A question needing ten steps of work does not get ten times the compute just because it is harder.

## Writing To Itself First

If you ask a human to solve a hard problem, they might talk to themselves inside their head, or get out a pen and paper and write out their reasoning. 

*Reasoning models* solve this problem in roughly the same way. The model writes out its thinking process in a series of hidden messages the human user can't see. 

<ThinkFirstWidget>
The dashed message is the model's working. It is generated one token at a time, exactly like the answer, and the model reads it back before answering.
</ThinkFirstWidget>

Nothing about the architecture changes. There is no new component and no extra network. The thinking tokens are predicted exactly like every other token, and the model reads them back the same way it reads anything else in its context. Its own output becomes its working memory.

## How They Are Trained

The obvious way to teach this would be to write out ideal reasoning and train on it, the way <Ch slug="chat" /> showed for ideal answers. That does not scale, because nobody can write good working for millions of problems.

What works instead needs only the verifiable rewards from the last chapter. Give the model a problem it can be marked on automatically, let it attempt the problem many times over, and check nothing but the final answer.

<TraceSamplingWidget>
Three attempts at one problem. The checker never reads the working, only the answer. The two that failed both did something reasonable and stopped too soon.
</TraceSamplingWidget>

Repeat that over millions of problems and the habits that survive are the ones that lead to correct answers: checking your work, backing out of a dead end, noticing halfway through that an earlier step was wrong. Nobody wrote those down, and nobody marked a single line of working.

This is also why reasoning helps most where answers can be checked. Maths and code improved dramatically. The habits carry over to other questions, but more weakly, because nothing was ever able to score those.

## Two Things To Keep Straight

The trace is not a complete account of what drove the answer. Give a model a hint of what answer you want, and it will often write a confident chain of reasoning that justifies the answer it thinks you want, without ever mentioning your hint. People do the same thing.

More thinking is also not always better. Every thinking token costs time and money, and on an easy question a long trace mostly gives the model more chances to talk itself out of the right answer.
