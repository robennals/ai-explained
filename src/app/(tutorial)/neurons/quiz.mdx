According to the chapter, a single neuron does just two things. They are:

<Choice>Memorizes facts and recalls them on demand</Choice>
<Choice correct>Weighted sum followed by an activation function</Choice>
<Choice>Compares two inputs and picks the larger one</Choice>
<Choice>Multiplies all its inputs together</Choice>

<Feedback>
A neuron does two operations: multiply each input by a weight, sum them up (plus a bias), then pass the result through an activation function. Multiplication, addition, and one function. Stack enough of these together and you get GPT or the human brain.

<Question>
In this chapter, the *sigmoid* is described as:

<Choice>A function that doubles its input</Choice>
<Choice correct>A curve that squashes numbers into 0–1</Choice>
<Choice>A way to randomly initialize the weights</Choice>
<Choice>The error signal that flows backward through layers</Choice>

<Feedback>
The sigmoid is an S-shape that smoothly turns any input number into something between 0 and 1. Large positive inputs become close to 1, large negative ones become close to 0, and middle values get smoothly graded. Think of it as a dimmer switch instead of a light switch.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
The chapter says hidden layers in a deep network might detect, in order: edges → shapes → eyes/ears → whole cats. What's the pattern?

<Choice>The network re-reads the original input at each layer</Choice>
<Choice correct>Each layer builds richer features from the previous</Choice>
<Choice>Hidden layers store memories from previous inputs</Choice>
<Choice>Layers reduce the number of weights needed</Choice>

<Feedback>
Each layer takes the previous layer's outputs and combines them into something more complex. Early layers detect simple things like edges; middle layers combine those into parts like eyes; later layers combine those into whole objects. Depth is what lets a network build up from simple features to complex concepts.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Why can't you train a network of logic gates using the optimization methods from Chapter 2?

<Choice>Logic gates are too small to add weights to</Choice>
<Choice>Logic gates can't compute non-binary arithmetic operations</Choice>
<Choice correct>Their outputs jump all-or-nothing with no smooth gradient</Choice>
<Choice>There aren't enough logic gates in modern computers</Choice>

<Feedback>
Logic gates jump from 0 to 1 with nothing in between, so there's no smooth gradient to follow. You can't ask "how should I adjust this gate to make the answer a little more correct?" — same problem you'd face trying to crack a password by tweaking guesses. Without a continuous signal, optimization has nothing to work with.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
A reader argues: "The bigger a neural network, the smarter it is. Just keep adding connections." How does the chapter complicate this view?

<Choice>Real AI hasn't actually become smarter recently</Choice>
<Choice>Bigger networks can't be trained with gradient descent</Choice>
<Choice correct>The quality of the weights matters most</Choice>
<Choice>Bigger networks always become unstable during training</Choice>

<Feedback>
Consider two examples: today's largest language models have roughly as many parameters as a mouse brain has synapses, yet they can write essays while mice can't. And elephants have *more* brain neurons than humans, without being smarter. What separates them is the quality of the connections — the weights — which is exactly what training optimizes.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
A neuron with two inputs (each 0 or 1) uses a sigmoid: the activation maps a big positive number to near 1, and a big negative number to near 0. Which weights and bias make it compute the AND gate (output near 1 only when both inputs are 1)?

<Choice>weights = (10, 10), bias = −5</Choice>
<Choice correct>weights = (10, 10), bias = −15</Choice>
<Choice>weights = (−10, −10), bias = 15</Choice>
<Choice>weights = (−10, 0), bias = 5</Choice>

<Feedback>
For AND, the sum should be positive only when both inputs are 1. With weights (10, 10), the sum is the bias when nothing fires, bias + 10 when one input fires, and bias + 20 when both fire. Bias = −15 makes the both-inputs sum = 5 (clearly positive → near 1) but the one-input sum = −5 (clearly negative → near 0). The big magnitudes make the sigmoid fire crisply — its output is very close to 0 or 1 rather than wishy-washy in the middle.

The other choices are real logic gates, just not AND. (A) bias = −5 fires whenever any input is 1 — that's OR. (C) weights = (−10, −10), bias = 15 fires *unless* both inputs are 1 — that's NAND. (D) weights = (−10, 0), bias = 5 ignores the second input and flips the first — that's NOT applied to the first input.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
The chapter shows three neurons in two layers solving XOR, while a single neuron can't. What does the second layer let you do that one layer alone can't?

<Choice>Train faster on the same data</Choice>
<Choice>Use a different activation function</Choice>
<Choice correct>Combine facts computed by hidden neurons</Choice>
<Choice>Reduce the number of weights needed</Choice>

<Feedback>
A single neuron is limited to one weighted sum followed by one activation — too simple for XOR. With two layers, the hidden neurons compute *intermediate facts* (say, OR and NAND), which the output neuron then combines. The output doesn't have to solve XOR on its own — it just needs to AND together what the hidden neurons figured out. That's what depth buys you.
</Feedback>
</Question>
