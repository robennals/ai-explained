<Question>
A *base model* is a model that has been pre-trained and nothing else. Compared to a chat model, it:

<Choice>Knows far fewer facts about the world</Choice>
<Choice correct>Knows almost as much, but continues documents instead of answering</Choice>
<Choice>Uses a different architecture with no attention</Choice>
<Choice>Cannot produce grammatical English</Choice>

<Feedback>
Nearly all of a model's knowledge comes from pre-training, so the base model already has it. What it lacks is the habit of treating your text as a request addressed to it. Ask a base model a question and it works out what kind of document your question appeared in, then continues that document, which is often a list of more questions.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
What does post-training mostly do?

<Choice>Adds the model's knowledge of facts</Choice>
<Choice>Replaces the transformer with a conversational architecture</Choice>
<Choice correct>Selects between behaviours the base model already has</Choice>
<Choice>Uses most of the total training compute</Choice>

<Feedback>
Post-training is a thin layer on top of pre-training, often less than 1% of the compute. It rarely teaches new facts. What it does is pick out, from all the behaviours latent in the base model, the ones that look like a helpful assistant answering a question.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
In RLHF, what is the reward model for?

<Choice>Generating the responses that get shown to users</Choice>
<Choice correct>Predicting the score a human rater would have given</Choice>
<Choice>Storing the conversation between turns</Choice>
<Choice>Checking arithmetic and running code</Choice>

<Feedback>
Human comparisons are expensive, so they are used once to train a second model that predicts how a rater would score a response. That reward model can then score huge numbers of responses no human ever saw. It is a model, so it can be wrong, and training hard against a wrong score is where a lot of odd behaviour comes from.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Post-training can reward the model for giving people answers they are happy with. What is the risk?

<Choice>There are not enough conversations to learn anything from</Choice>
<Choice>It only works for questions with a checkable answer</Choice>
<Choice>Someone has to read every conversation for it to be usable</Choice>
<Choice correct>Being pleased with an answer is not the same as the answer being right</Choice>

<Feedback>
Real conversations are the biggest source of feedback there is, and it costs nothing: whether the next message says thanks, or complains that the question was not answered, or asks the same thing again more sharply. What it measures is whether the person was pleased, which is only a stand-in for whether the answer was good. A confident wrong answer often pleases; a correct answer someone did not want to hear often does not. Lean on this signal too hard and the model learns to flatter, a failure mode called sycophancy.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
The system prompt is:

<Choice>A separate model that supervises the main one</Choice>
<Choice>A set of hard constraints enforced outside the model</Choice>
<Choice correct>Text placed at the top of the same token stream the model is completing</Choice>
<Choice>A list of weights loaded before the conversation starts</Choice>

<Feedback>
Underneath a chat interface there is one document with role markers in it. The system prompt is text at the top of that document. That is why it works, since the model is completing the document and everything in it shapes what comes next, and also why it is not a hard guarantee.
</Feedback>
</Question>
