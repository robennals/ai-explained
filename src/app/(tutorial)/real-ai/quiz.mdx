<Question>
Today's frontier models are mostly:

<Choice>A new design that replaced the transformer</Choice>
<Choice correct>The transformer design, scaled up and tweaked</Choice>
<Choice>Many small models that vote on each answer</Choice>
<Choice>Transformers with the attention mechanism taken out</Choice>

<Feedback>
The models behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are built from attention, positions, layers, and next-word prediction, exactly as before. What changed is the size, the training, and a set of individually simple additions bolted on top. That's why understanding the core machine gets you most of the way to understanding the frontier.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
In a mixture-of-experts model, what does the router do?

<Choice>Splits the model across several computers</Choice>
<Choice>Decides which language the answer is in</Choice>
<Choice correct>Picks a few experts for each word</Choice>
<Choice>Chooses how long the answer should be</Choice>

<Feedback>
Instead of one thinking layer that every word passes through, a mixture-of-experts model has many smaller ones. The router looks at each word and wakes only a few of them. DeepSeek-V3 has 256 experts and uses 8 per word, so the model can hold an enormous amount of knowledge while only paying for a slice of it at a time.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Nobody outside OpenAI or Anthropic knows how GPT-5 or Claude are built. So why is it still possible to make a reasonable guess?

<Choice>Those companies publish their architectures in full</Choice>
<Choice>A model's answers reveal how many layers it has</Choice>
<Choice correct>Open models that score similarly publish their designs</Choice>
<Choice>Every neural network must be built the same way</Choice>

<Feedback>
Several models with published blueprints score close to the closed ones. GLM-5 reports 77.8 on a benchmark of fixing real bugs, against GPT-5's 74.9. So we have full build instructions for something roughly as capable, which makes it likely the secret models work along similar lines.

It's worth being careful about how strong that claim is. It's an inference, not a fact. Nobody outside those labs can check, and a company that had found something genuinely new would have every reason to keep quiet about it.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
A lab has an enormous pile of raw text scraped from the web and a fixed budget. Based on how modern models are trained, what would most improve the result?

<Choice>Training on all of it for as many passes as possible</Choice>
<Choice correct>Throwing most of it out and keeping the best</Choice>
<Choice>Sorting it alphabetically before training begins</Choice>
<Choice>Removing all the computer code from the mixture</Choice>

<Feedback>
Most of the web is junk, and training on junk teaches a model to predict junk. One documented pipeline cut 36 trillion words down to 15 trillion, and filters as crude as "most lines in this document are very short" removed about a fifth of the text while making the resulting model better.

Removing the code would actively hurt. Code is a small fraction of the internet but around 17% of what Llama 3 was trained on, because working through code appears to make models better at reasoning generally.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
A new model has a trillion parameters, several times more than an older one. What does that tell you about the work it does for each word?

<Choice>It does several times more work per word</Choice>
<Choice correct>Little, since it may use only a fraction</Choice>
<Choice>It must be several times slower to answer</Choice>
<Choice>It needs several times as much training data</Choice>

<Feedback>
Mixture of experts broke the link between how much a model knows and how much it costs to think. Kimi K2 stores about a trillion parameters and switches on 32 billion of them per word. gpt-oss stores 117 billion and uses 5.1 billion. Both use fewer per word than GPT-3, which had 175 billion and ran every one of them for every word it read.

The tempting assumption is that parameter count and running cost move together. For a model without experts that's true, and it was true for years, which is exactly why the change is easy to miss.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
Which statement about the cost of running AI is accurate?

<Choice>Running AI of every kind keeps getting cheaper</Choice>
<Choice>Falling prices are entirely due to better algorithms</Choice>
<Choice correct>A fixed level of ability keeps getting cheaper</Choice>
<Choice>The cost of the newest models barely changes</Choice>

<Feedback>
Getting GPT-3.5-level answers became more than 280 times cheaper in about two years. But running the newest models is getting *more* expensive each year, because they are larger and think for longer before answering. Cheap and frontier move in opposite directions, so the claim has to be pinned to a fixed level of ability to be true.

The price drop also isn't purely an algorithmic achievement. A good share of it is hardware getting cheaper, and some is simply companies competing on price rather than anything becoming more efficient.
</Feedback>
</Question>

<Question>
People preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter aligned model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. What does that suggest?

<Choice>Smaller models are usually more capable than big ones</Choice>
<Choice>Size stopped mattering once alignment was invented</Choice>
<Choice>People cannot reliably tell models apart at all</Choice>
<Choice correct>Teaching a model to be useful can outweigh size</Choice>

<Feedback>
A raw pre-trained model has learned to continue text, not to help you. Ask it a question and a perfectly good continuation is a list of similar questions. Alignment is what turns that into an assistant, and here it was worth more than a hundredfold difference in size.

This doesn't mean small models beat large ones in general. The 1.3-billion-parameter model knew far less. It simply used what it knew to answer the question that was actually asked, which turned out to matter more to the people judging.
</Feedback>
</Question>
