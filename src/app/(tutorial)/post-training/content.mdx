The previous chapter ended with a machine that predicts one token. That machine is not ChatGPT or Claude. This chapter is about the training that turns it into something useful.

## Prompts and Completions

The transformer in <Ch slug="transformers" /> predicts a single token. Given a piece of text, it says which token plausibly comes next. Run it once and you get a single word. Add that word to the end of the text and run it again, and again, and what comes out is a whole block of text.

That block is called a *completion*, and the text you started it from is called the *prompt*. Hand a model a prompt, get back a completion. That is the whole of what a language model does.

A prompt does not have to be a question, or an instruction, or anything addressed to anybody. It is just text that stops.

## What a Raw Model Does

A model trained to predict what comes next for any body of text, and trained to do nothing else, is called a *base model*. It has read an enormous amount of text (<Ch slug="next-word-prediction" />), and it has picked up enough about how the world works to write a plausible completion for almost any prompt you give it.

Often a base model will write exactly what you hoped for. Ask it how to stop a sourdough starter going mouldy and it may well give a good answer, because pages that follow a question with a good answer are part of what it has seen. But it has also seen lists of questions, and other things that aren't what you want.

The base model is not confused and it is not stupid. It is doing exactly what we trained it to do. What we trained it to do was predict the next token, not to be a useful assistant.

So the useful answer is already in there. It is one of the things the model might write. It is just mixed in with a lot of completions that are less useful.

## Post-Training

Closing that gap is a whole second stage of training. The two stages have names:

- **Pre-training** is next-word prediction over a very large body of text. It is where the model picks up its understanding of how the world works. Nearly all of the compute goes here, often more than 99% of it.
- **Post-training** is everything after that. It teaches the model how to apply that understanding to the thing you actually want the model to do. It is a thin layer, and it accounts for the difference between the completions above and the assistant you are used to.

It is worth being clear about what post-training is not. The model has already read a great deal of real back-and-forth conversation, and a great many genuinely helpful answers. It can already produce the kind of response you want. That response is simply not the most likely thing it will say, and not what it will pick when left to itself. Post-training does not teach the model a new kind of completion. It steers it towards the completions we want and away from the ones we do not.

There are several ways to do that, and a modern chat model has usually been through most of them. Most work the same way underneath: produce several candidate responses, score them somehow, and adjust the model's weights so that higher-scoring responses become more likely.

Post-training does not teach the model new facts or new ways of understanding the world. It steers it towards using what it already has in the way we want.

## Putting It Together

A base model and a chat model are the same machine, and they know almost exactly the same things. What separates them is a thin layer of training that made the helpful answer the likely one.

That is half of what turns a next word predictor into something you can talk to. The other half is not training at all. <Ch slug="conversations" /> is about the plumbing: how a back-and-forth conversation gets turned into the single piece of text a model can complete.
