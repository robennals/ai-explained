# Making Training Work Well

<Lead>
The last chapter made training sound easy. Nudge every weight the right way, and
the network learns. In theory, a huge network is no harder to train than a tiny
one.
</Lead>

In practice, it is much harder. Trained the simple way, a deep network often
learns almost nothing.

This chapter is a tour of the tricks that fix that. None of them is complicated.
Each one solves a different way that training goes wrong.

## Getting the message back to every layer

Training works by feedback. The network makes a guess. We measure how wrong it
was. Then each weight gets a message telling it which way to change.

For weights near the end, the message is clear. For weights deep inside, it has to
travel back through many layers. Each layer makes it fainter.

Think about why your soccer team won on Saturday. Partly the third goal. Partly the
striker being in the right spot. Partly a good night's sleep. Partly a quiet hotel
the night before.

Follow the chain back far enough and the last reason barely counts. A deep network
has the same trouble. Its earliest layers get a message too faint to use.

Every playground below is built from the same tiny machine: one neuron. It has an
input, a knob or two, and an output. You give it a target and press a button to
make it learn one step. Then you watch whether it actually moves.

### Pick an activation that can learn

Every neuron passes its number through an activation. The old favorite is the
sigmoid, an S-shaped curve. At the ends, the curve goes flat. A neuron sitting on
a flat part barely responds, so it barely learns.

It is like a volume knob that maxes out. Past a point, turning it does nothing.

ReLU works better. It blocks negative numbers and lets positive ones through at
full strength, with no ceiling. It never goes deaf the way a sigmoid does.

You might worry that letting numbers grow with no ceiling would send them out of
control. It can, but a later trick called normalization keeps everything in a
sensible range, so in practice it works out fine.

### Start in a good place

Where a neuron begins matters too. Start it with numbers that are too big, and it
lands out on the flat part before it has learned anything. Good starting values
keep it in the responsive middle.

<BadInitWidget>
Press **Bad initialization**, then take a few steps. The neuron barely moves. Press
**Good initialization** and try again. Same neuron, but now it learns fast. The
only difference is where it started. Click around the graph to feel where learning
is quick and where it stalls.
</BadInitWidget>

Starting values have a second job. If two neurons in a layer start exactly the
same, they get the same message and change in lockstep. They stay identical twins
forever, and the whole layer does the work of one neuron. The fix is to start each
neuron a little differently, at random.

<TwinsWidget>
Take a few steps and watch the two neurons move together. They started the same, so
they stay the same. Now press **Randomize neuron B**. They split apart, and the
layer can finally learn two different things.
</TwinsWidget>

### Give the message a shortcut

The deeper the network, the fainter the message to its early layers. Residual
connections fix this. Instead of each layer replacing the work before it, each
layer adds to it.

Picture a group writing one document. If each person adds their own paragraph, you
can see who wrote what. Find a bad paragraph, and you know who to ask to fix it.

But if each person rewrites the whole thing, mistakes blur together and you cannot
tell who caused what. A plain deep network is the second kind. Residual connections
make it the first.

<ResidualStreamDiagram />

Because each layer adds instead of replacing, nothing gets lost. The network stays
deep and powerful, but now every layer has a direct line to the answer. This is the
residual stream from the transformers chapter.

<VanishingChainWidget>

to life.
</VanishingChainWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Almost every trick here has one job: keep the learning message strong as it travels
back through a deep network, so it never fades to nothing or blows up.
</KeyInsight>

## Learning from small groups at a time

To take one perfect step, you would check every example first. With millions of
them, that is far too slow. Checking just one example is fast but jumpy.

It is the soccer season again. Do not wait until the season ends to improve. Review
after each game. But do not panic over one rainy game with a lucky bounce either. A
few recent games tell you enough.

So we learn from small groups, called batches. Here is the surprise: a batch of one
with tiny steps ends up almost as accurate as using everything at once.

The real reason to batch is speed. A GPU can handle hundreds of examples at the same
time, about as fast as one. So a batch gives a good direction cheaply and gets there
in far fewer steps.

<BatchingWidget>
Drag the batch size. Small batches take many jumpy steps. Big batches take a few
smooth ones. The hardware keeps the cost per step low until the batch gets very
large.
</BatchingWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Batches are not about a more perfect step. They are about using fast hardware to get
a good-enough step in as few tries as possible.
</KeyInsight>

## Keeping the numbers in a sensible range

As a network trains, its numbers drift. Some grow huge, some shrink to nothing. Each
layer keeps having to adjust to the mess coming in.

Normalization cleans this up. It is like grading on a curve. A raw score of 70 means
nothing until you compare it to the rest of the class. Then it is clear.

Normalization rescales each layer's numbers to a standard range on every pass. The
next layer always knows what to expect. This is also what stops ReLU's numbers from
growing out of control.

There are two common kinds. Batch norm compares each value to the other examples in
the group. Layer norm compares each value to the others in the same example. Layer
norm does not care how big the group is, so transformers use it.

<NormalizationWidget>
Drag the numbers in three example vectors. In **layer norm**, each vector is rescaled
on its own. In **batch norm**, each feature is rescaled across the group. Switch
between them to see the difference.
</NormalizationWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Normalization keeps every layer's numbers in a steady range, so the whole network can
learn at one sensible speed.
</KeyInsight>

## Choosing how big a step to take

One step size cannot be right for the whole trip. Some directions are steep, some are
nearly flat. A single setting either overshoots the steep parts or crawls along the
flat ones.

Think about getting from your house to a friend's living room across the world. You do
not move at one speed. You back out of the driveway slowly, fly fast across the ocean,
then take tiny steps down the hallway to the right door.

A few ideas help. Starting slow, called warmup, sounds odd when you have so far to go.
But the weights start out random, so one big early step can throw the network somewhere
it never recovers from.

Momentum keeps you rolling in a steady direction, so small bumps do not knock you
around. And Adam gives each weight its own step size: it shrinks the steps that keep
overshooting and grows the ones that crawl. Almost every big model uses it.

<OptimizerRaceWidget>
Race plain steps, momentum, and Adam down the same hill. Too small a step and it never
arrives. Too big and it flies off. Adam tunes itself and usually wins.
</OptimizerRaceWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Adam is momentum plus a step size that tunes itself for every weight. That is what lets
huge models train without anyone setting speeds by hand.
</KeyInsight>

## Learning instead of memorizing

A big network can get every training answer right by memorizing, noise and all. Then it
fails on anything new. What we really want is for it to do well on data it has never
seen.

It is the difference between understanding math and memorizing last year's answer key.
The answer key gets a perfect score on the old test and falls apart on the new one.

Dropout fights this. Do not learn a cat only by its fur, or only by its ears. It might
be hairless, or wearing a hat. Dropout randomly hides some clues while training, so the
network learns to use many.

Weight decay helps too. It nudges the weights to stay small, so the network keeps its
explanation simple.

<OverfittingWidget>

<KeyInsight>
A low training score was never the goal. Doing well on new data is. Some tricks make
training harder on purpose to get there.
</KeyInsight>

## Putting it together

The simple way to train barely works. The message fades, the numbers drift, the steps
are the wrong size, and the network would rather memorize than learn.

Each trick here fixes one of those problems. Stack them together and you can train
networks with billions of weights.

One honest note: a lot of this was found by trial and error. We know these tricks help.
We are still working out exactly why some of them work so well.
