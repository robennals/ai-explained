# Why Training Almost Doesn't Work

<Lead>
The neurons chapter made training sound easy. Every weight has a gradient, so
you nudge them all downhill at once and the network learns. A network with ten
thousand weights, it said, is no harder to train in principle than one with ten.
That is true in principle. In practice, a plain deep network trained this way
barely works at all. It stalls, or blows up, or memorizes its training data and
fails on everything else.
</Lead>

This chapter is a tour of the tricks that close the gap between principle and
practice. None of them is complicated on its own. Each one fixes a specific way
that naive training goes wrong. Taken together, they are the difference between a
network that learns nothing and the models that write, draw, and reason today.

## The learning signal has to survive the trip back

Training is credit assignment. The network makes a prediction, we measure how
wrong it was, and then every weight gets a signal telling it which way to move to
make the error smaller. For a weight near the output, that signal is clear. For a
weight buried deep in the network, it has to travel back through every layer
above it, and at each step it gets multiplied by another number. Multiply enough
numbers smaller than one together and the signal shrinks to nothing. Multiply
numbers larger than one and it explodes.

Think about tracing why your soccer team won on Saturday. Partly because of the
third goal. Which happened partly because the striker was in the right place.
Partly because she was fresh. Partly because she slept well. Partly because the
hotel was quiet. Partly because... By the time you have followed the chain back
far enough, the link between "we won" and "the hotel was quiet" is so faint you
cannot act on it. A deep network has the same problem in reverse: the earliest
layers are the hotel, and the signal reaching them is too weak to be useful.

To see this up close, the playgrounds below all share the same tiny machine: a
single neuron with an input, a weight, a bias, and an activation. It has a
current output, a target we want it to reach, and the error between them. Press
its button to run one learning step and watch whether the output actually moves
toward the target. That is training, stripped down to one moving part.

### An activation that can actually learn

The neurons chapter used the sigmoid, an S-shaped curve that squeezes any number
into the range 0 to 1. That squeeze has a cost. Out at the ends of the S the
curve goes flat, and a neuron sitting on a flat part has almost no slope. A
learning step multiplies by that slope, so a saturated neuron barely budges no
matter how wrong it is.

A sigmoid is like a volume knob that maxes out. Past a certain point you keep
turning and nothing gets louder. A neuron stuck out there has stopped listening.
ReLU behaves more like a one-way valve. It blocks anything negative, passing
nothing, but lets positive signal straight through at full strength with no
ceiling, so it never goes deaf the way a sigmoid does. That single change is a
big part of why deep networks started working. (Modern networks often use smooth
cousins of ReLU, such as Swish and GELU, but the idea is the same.)

slope is near zero, and that is exactly when learning stalls.
</ActivationWidget>

### Starting in the right place

Where a neuron begins matters as much as which activation it uses. Start it with
weights that are too large and it lands out on the flat part of its curve before
it has learned anything, saturated from the first step. Initialization is the art
of picking starting weights at a scale that keeps every neuron in its responsive
range, so the signal neither shrinks nor blows up as it crosses each layer.

<BadInitWidget>
Press **Bad initialization**, then take a few learning steps. The neuron is stuck
out on the flat part and barely moves. Now press **Good initialization** and try
again. Same neuron, same target, but now it learns quickly. The only difference
is where it started.
</BadInitWidget>

Initialization has a second, sneakier job. If every neuron in a layer starts with
the same weights, they all get the same learning signal and move together forever,
staying identical twins. A layer of a hundred twins does the work of one neuron.
The fix is to start every neuron with different random weights, so they can drift
apart and specialize.

<TwinsWidget>
Take a few learning steps and watch the two neurons move in perfect lockstep.
They started identical, so they stay identical. Now press **Randomize neuron B**
and keep going. Once they differ, they can learn different things and the layer
finally uses its full capacity.
</TwinsWidget>

### Giving the signal a shortcut

The deeper the network, the fainter the learning signal reaching its early layers.
Residual connections fix this with a simple move: instead of each layer replacing
what came before, it adds its contribution on top.

Picture a document that many people work on. If each person adds their own
paragraph, then when you find a bad paragraph later you know exactly who wrote it
and can ask them to fix it. But if each person instead rewrites the whole document
from the previous version, a mistake gets smeared across everyone, and the person
who first introduced it is impossible to single out. A plain deep network is the
second kind. Residual connections make it the first.

<ResidualStreamDiagram />

Because each layer adds rather than overwrites, information accumulates instead of
being buried, so the network keeps all the depth and power it had. What it gains
is a direct path from every layer to the output, which means a deep layer can
contribute directly and be corrected directly when it is wrong. This shared,
added-to document is the residual stream from the transformers chapter: every
attention and feed-forward block reads it and adds back to it.

Each bar shows how much that neuron actually learns in one step. With a long plain
chain, the earliest neuron (the red bar) barely moves. Increase the **depth** and
watch it shrink further. Now switch on **residual connections** and the early bars
come back to life. The signal has a shortcut home.
</VanishingChainWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Most of the architecture tricks in deep learning exist to keep one number under
control: the strength of the learning signal as it travels back through depth.
Activations, initialization, and residual connections are three different ways to
stop it from fading to nothing or blowing up.
</KeyInsight>

## Learning from a handful of examples at a time

To take one honest step, you would measure the error on the entire training set,
all the way through, and only then update. With millions of examples that is far
too slow. The opposite extreme, updating after every single example, is fast but
jumpy: one weird example yanks the network in the wrong direction.

This is the soccer season again. You do not wait until the final whistle of the
season to think about how to play better, and you also do not overhaul your whole
strategy after one rainy away game with a lucky bounce. You review your last few
games together. A small batch of examples gives a good-enough sense of direction
without overreacting to any single one.

Here is the surprising part. Using a batch of one with a small step is almost as
accurate as using the whole dataset at once, because the direction works out about
the same either way. The real reason to use batches is the hardware. A GPU can
work through hundreds of examples at the same time, in roughly the time it takes
to do one. So a batch of 256 gets a far better sense of direction for almost the
same wall-clock cost, and reaches the finish line in a fraction of the steps.

<BatchingWidget>
Drag the batch size. Small batches take many jumpy steps; large batches take few
smooth ones. Watch how the parallel hardware keeps the cost per step almost flat
until the batch gets very large.
</BatchingWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Batching is not about computing a more correct gradient than a single example
could. It is about using parallel hardware to get a good-enough direction in as
few steps as possible.
</KeyInsight>

## Keeping the numbers in a sane range

Even with a careful start, the numbers flowing through a network drift as it
trains. Weights change, so the scale of each layer's inputs wanders, and every
layer keeps having to re-adapt to its inputs' shifting range. That drift slows
everything down and forces the learning rate to stay tiny.

Normalization keeps the numbers civilized. It is grading on a curve. A raw score
of 70 means nothing until you know how everyone else did; once you standardize it,
the next person always knows what to expect. Normalization does the same to a
layer's outputs, rescaling them to a standard spread on every pass, then letting
the network stretch them back with a couple of learned knobs.

There are two common versions, and the difference is what you standardize against.
Batch normalization grades each student against everyone else who sat the exam in
the same room, which needs a big, steady roomful to be reliable. It works well for
image models. Layer normalization grades each student against their own other
answers, ignoring the room entirely. That makes it independent of batch size and
of how many words are in the sentence, and it behaves the same whether the model
is training or generating one word at a time. That is why transformers use it.

<NormalizationWidget>
Drag the raw values of three example vectors. In **layer norm** mode each vector
is standardized on its own. Switch to **batch norm** mode and each feature is
standardized down the batch instead. Watch which direction the rescaling runs.
</NormalizationWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Normalization keeps every layer's inputs in a predictable range so the whole
network can train at one sensible learning rate instead of each layer chasing a
moving target.
</KeyInsight>

## How big a step to take

A single step size cannot be right for the whole journey. Some directions in the
landscape are steep and some are nearly flat, and one setting either bounces
across the steep parts or crawls along the flat ones.

Think of getting from your home in San Francisco to a friend's living room in
Paris. You do not travel at one speed. You ease carefully out of the driveway
while you get your bearings, cruise at full speed across the country and the ocean,
and then take tiny careful steps down the hallway to the right door. You also match
your speed to the terrain, sprinting the open highway and inching through a crowded
hall.

A few ideas make this work. Starting slow, called warmup, sounds backwards when
you have so far to go, but at the start the weights are random and the direction is
barely trustworthy, so a big confident step can fling the model somewhere it never
recovers from. Momentum keeps a running average of recent steps, so the model
builds speed in a consistent direction and is not knocked around by every bump.
And Adam, the optimizer nearly all large models use, gives each weight its own step
size by tracking how big that weight's recent gradients have been and dividing its
step down when they are large. A weight that keeps overshooting gets reined in; one
that has been crawling gets a push.

<OptimizerRaceWidget>
Race plain gradient descent against momentum and Adam down the same landscape. Try
the **learning rate** too small and nothing arrives; too large and it overshoots
and diverges; just right and it settles. Adam adapts its step per direction and
usually gets there with the least fuss.
</OptimizerRaceWidget>

<KeyInsight>
Adam is momentum plus a separate, self-adjusting step size for every weight.
Adapting the step to the terrain, direction by direction, is what lets enormous
models train without anyone tuning thousands of learning rates by hand.
</KeyInsight>

## Learning versus memorizing

A big enough network can drive its training error to zero by memorizing the
answers, noise and all. It looks perfect in practice and then falls apart on
anything it has not seen. What we actually care about is how it does on new data,
not on the examples it studied.

This is the difference between understanding the material and cramming last year's
answer key. The key gets you a perfect score on last year's paper and leaves you
lost on this year's. The tricks that fight it all work by making training a little
harder on purpose.

Dropout is the clearest example. Do not learn to recognize a cat only by its fur,
or only by its ears. It might be a hairless cat, or one wearing a hat. During
training, dropout randomly hides some of the network's cues, forcing it to rely on
many independent ones, so it still copes when a few are missing. Weight decay pulls
in the same direction by gently pushing weights toward zero, so the network prefers
the simplest explanation that fits, the way a good scientist distrusts a needlessly
elaborate theory.

<KeyInsight>
Low training error was never the goal. Generalization is. Several of training's
most important tricks deliberately make the network's job harder so that what it
learns holds up on data it has never seen.
</KeyInsight>

## What's next

Plain gradient descent almost does not work on a deep network. The signal vanishes
or explodes on its way back, the numbers drift out of range, the step size is
wrong for the terrain, and the model would rather memorize than learn. Each trick
in this chapter answers one of those failures, and stacked together they are why we
can train networks with billions of weights at all.

It is worth being honest that a lot of this was found by trial and error. We know
that residual connections and Adam and normalization help enormously, but the full
story of why some of them work so well is still being written.
